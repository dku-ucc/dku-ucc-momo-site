# Speaker


## Chew Soo Hong


Professor Chew Soo Hong is Professor at Southwestern University of Finance and Economics and Emeritus Professor at the National University of Singapore.


He is a Fellow of the Econometric Society and a Fellow of the Society for the Advancement of Economic Theory.


Professor Chew is recognized as one of the pioneers of axiomatic non-expected utility models.


---

# Research Background


Professor Chew's first published paper:

*"A Generalization of the Quasilinear Mean with Application to the Measurement of Income Inequality and Decision Theory Resolving the Allais Paradox"*  
(*Econometrica*, 1983)


was among the earliest works proposing alternatives to the expected utility model that forms the foundation of modern decision theory established by John von Neumann and Oskar Morgenstern.


His research investigates the multifaceted nature of decision-making, including:

- Temporal choice
- Mechanism design
- Behavioral economics
- Experimental economics
- Neuroeconomic approaches


He has also explored decision-making processes through interdisciplinary methods including neuroimaging and genomics.


---

# Talk Overview


Traditional decision theory often assumes that individuals process available information in a fully rational and comprehensive manner.


In this talk, Professor Chew examines decision-making through the perspective of a **cognitive miser**, exploring how limited attention influences choices under risk and uncertainty.


Drawing on his research on **Attention Theory (AT)**, Professor Chew studies how attention allocation affects behavioral patterns that differ from predictions based on expected utility models.


---

# Attention Theory and Decision Making


The research considers multiple dimensions of attention, including:

- Bottom-up attention
- Top-down attention


Attention Theory provides a framework for explaining a range of non-expected utility behavioral patterns, including:


- Allais paradox
- Ambiguity aversion
- Differences in loss-gain attitudes
- Status quo bias
- Default effects and endowment effects


Through integrating behavioral economics, cognitive science, and biological approaches, this research provides a broader understanding of how humans make decisions in complex environments.
