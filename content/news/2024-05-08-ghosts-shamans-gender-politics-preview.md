# Ghosts, Shamans, and Gender Politics in China, 1942–1953


![Event Poster](/images/events/ghosts-shamans-gender-politics-2025/poster.png)


The Duke Kunshan University Colloquium Committee (UCC) presents:

**"Ghosts, Shamans, and Gender Politics in China, 1942–1953"**

featuring Professor Xiaofei Kang from George Washington University.


This talk examines the relationship between religion, gender, and political transformation during the Chinese Communist revolution, focusing on the anti-superstition campaigns from 1942 to 1953.


By placing religion and gender at the center of revolutionary history, Professor Kang explores how traditional beliefs, symbolic systems, and gendered narratives were transformed and reinterpreted during a period of profound social change.

# Talk Overview


The talk moves religion and gender to the center of the study of the Chinese revolution by examining the mobilizational dynamics of anti-superstition campaigns from 1942 to 1953.


Professor Kang argues that religion was not simply an obstacle for revolutionaries. Instead, religious traditions also provided models through which mass mobilization and political legitimacy could be constructed.


The talk explores how traditional cosmological concepts, including the forces of yin and yang that shaped gender hierarchy and ritual order, were reinterpreted in revolutionary contexts.


It further examines how revolutionary art and literature transformed older narratives of female ghosts and ritual exorcism into new political symbols, creating a masculinist vision associated with scientific authority and revolutionary legitimacy.


Through these examples, Professor Kang demonstrates how gendered language and symbolism from Chinese religious traditions remained central to generating emotional, ethical, and intellectual appeals during revolutionary transformation.
